Material flows and domestic material consumption of Italy, Portugal and Sweden, from nation to municipality, calculated with the Urban Metabolism Analyst (UMAn).

This report brings together the UMAN results for the three ECLECTIC countries at every scale for which they could be calculated: Sweden from the nation down to the municipality of Göteborg (five levels), Italy and the Autonomous Province of Bolzano, and Portugal, Centro and Região de Coimbra. Seven "rest-of" territories (each parent minus its child) complete the picture. Every territory follows the same template, so material use can be compared across scales.

Main messages (2019):

  1. National levels differ by a factor of three: Sweden 21.9 t of DMC per capita, Portugal 10.9, Italy 7.6. The ranking follows domestic extraction per capita (21.5, 7.8 and 4.4 t).
  2. In Sweden, DMC per capita hardly depends on scale (19.1–23.0 t/cap from nation to municipality). What changes with scale is openness: the share of material inputs that come from outside the territory rises from 28% (Sweden) to 47% (Södra Sverige), about 60% (Västsverige, Västra Götaland) and 84% (Göteborg).
  3. Regions can differ strongly from their country: Bolzano consumes 20.8 t/cap against 7.5 t/cap in the rest of Italy; Centro 12.8 against 10.3 in the rest of Portugal. Both are extraction regions (wood and fruit; stone).
  4. Non-metallic minerals dominate DMC almost everywhere (35–67%), mainly crushed and building stone; biomass comes second. Bolzano is the exception (biomass 52%). Fossil fuels weigh most where refineries and energy imports are (Italy 30%, Göteborg 23%, rest of Portugal 21%).
  5. Uncertainty grows as the territory shrinks. The unbalanced share is 2% for Sweden and 19% for Göteborg, and the smallest rest-of territories carry negative cells of up to 14% of their DMC.

What the Urban Metabolism Analyst does

Cities depend on a continuous supply of materials. Resources enter a territory, are transformed and consumed, circulate between activities, accumulate in buildings and infrastructure, and leave as products, emissions or waste. Material Flow Analysis (MFA) measures these flows; the Urban Metabolism Analyst (UMAn), developed by Rosado and colleagues, follows them product by product and links them to the economic activities that use and transform them. This makes it possible to tell which products and activities drive the material use of a territory, which is what circular-economy actions target.

A product-level metabolism needs more than imports minus exports: products can be transformed several times inside the territory before they are consumed or leave it. UMAn reconstructs these transformations in eight steps (ECLECTIC deliverable D1.3, Monitoring scheme for Circular Economy action plans in cities):

  1. Product classification. All flows are expressed in the Combined Nomenclature (CN, about 10 000 products, 1 000 headings, 100 chapters), distinguishing final, semi-finished and intermediate products.
  2. Territorial flows. Domestic extraction (DE), international imports (M) and exports (X), and domestic inflows (NI) and outflows (NO) from and to the rest of the country. Their balance is the available resources: AR = DE + M − X + NI − NO.
  3. Products to activities. CN products are linked to NACE activities: production statistics tell which industries make which products; detailed trade data tell which industries use which inputs.
  4. Product transformations. Products are followed through successive production steps, e.g. fresh tomatoes (CN 0702) → crushed tomatoes (CN 2002, NACE 10.39) → frozen pizza (CN 1905, NACE 10.85).
  5. Iterate until flows balance. The transformation is repeated (the flush) until every tonne is consumed locally, transformed, kept in stock, exported or sent to the rest of the country.
  6. Product-level consumption. The result is the domestic material consumption (DMC) by product: what is actually consumed in the territory rather than the gross quantities entering it.
  7. Validate and reconcile. Classifications are aligned, gaps filled with documented assumptions (jobs, firms, relationships observed elsewhere) and uncertainty reported.
  8. Material categories. Products are converted into material groups (biomass, metals, non-metallic minerals, fossil, chemicals) and compared with economy-wide MFA statistics.

Products can reach final use directly or after one or more industrial transformations (D1.3). Linking CN products to NACE activities avoids counting the same tomato twice.

How the steps are implemented in this report

  • Accounts (steps 1–2). For every CN code (CN8; CN4 for Italy) and year: DE, M, X, NI, NO and, where ports matter, transit (MT/XT). AR = DE + M − X + NI − NO + MT − XT.
  • Flush (steps 3–5). In each round, part of each product goes to final use (households, government, investment) and part to industries. Industries consume it (sink: energy, combustion), keep it (static: same CN4 in and out) or transform it into other products, which enter the next round. Exports exceeding what entered (negative AR) are matched against what industries make (netted). Whatever cannot be matched is unbalanced: DMC = AR + unbalanced.
  • Structure. Who uses which product comes from firm imports by industry; what industries make from PRODCOM or industry–product pairs; local industry size from jobs. A known limitation (D1.3): inputs are estimated from imports, so locally sourced inputs (e.g. grapes for local wine) can be underestimated.
  • Tiers. A own accounts; B inputs downscaled from the parent by jobs; C the parent's DMC downscaled by consumer. Full method: 01_standard_model.md; data requirements: 13_data_requirements.md.
  • Rest-of territories are parent − child for every CN code and year (no new runs).

Findings

  • DMC is 457.2 Mt or 7.6 t/cap in 2019, the lowest per capita value in the report. It ranges from 7.2 to 8.2 t/cap over 2013–2021 and is highest in 2021.

Bolzano / South Tyrol (ITH10)

Italy · NUTS 2 = NUTS 3 · code ITH10 · tier A · years 2013–2021

Findings

  • DMC is spread over many products: 36 CN4 codes make up 80%. Crushed stone (18%) and apples (12%) lead.
  • Employment points to tourism: accommodation is the largest NACE division (13% of persons employed).
  • Caveats: trade tonnes are estimated from € values; the use structure is borrowed from Portugal; rail on the Brenner corridor is missing. The unbalanced share is low (1.3%).

Comparison across scales

Method · Italy · Portugal · Sweden · Comparison · About

  1. National DMC per capita follows extraction. Sweden (21.9 t/cap), Portugal (10.9) and Italy (7.6) rank as their domestic extraction per capita does (21.5, 7.8, 4.4). Italy compensates for low extraction with imports (54% dependency).
  2. Scale changes openness, not intensity (Sweden). DMC per capita stays between 19 and 23 t/cap at every Swedish scale and in every rest-of territory. Import dependency rises steadily as the territory shrinks: 28% (Sweden), 47% (SE2), 60% (SE23), 61% (SE232), 84% (Göteborg). International dependency follows (28% → 54%).
  3. City and hinterland differ in kind. Göteborg (19.1 t/cap) consumes less per capita than the rest of Västra Götaland (22.2), and differently: more fossil (23%) and metals (18%), less stone. The city handles domestic flows of more than twice its DMC; the hinterland extracts and refines.
  4. Regions can be far from their country. Bolzano (20.8 t/cap) against the rest of Italy (7.5), and Centro (12.8) against the rest of Portugal (10.3). In both cases the region extracts more per capita than the country (wood and fruit; stone) and ships part of it to the rest of the country.
  5. Composition is local. Non-metallic minerals are the largest group everywhere except Bolzano, where biomass leads (52%). Fossil materials concentrate where refineries and energy ports are: Italy 30%, Göteborg 23%, rest of Portugal 21%, West Sweden 15%. Elsewhere they are 4–9%.
  6. Results get less certain at smaller scales. The unbalanced share rises from 2% (Sweden) to 5% (regions) and 19% (Göteborg). Rest-of differences are reliable for large remainders (rest of Italy, rest of Sweden: negative cells ≤1.5% of DMC). They become fragile for small ones (rest of VGR 6.4%, Halland 14%). Small rest-of territories need runs of their own before their numbers are used.
  7. Downscaling hides local profiles. Coimbra (tier C, 12.1 t/cap) is a scaled copy of Centro. The Swedish municipal results show how different a local profile can be from its region's (Göteborg against VGR), so tier C values should be treated as placeholders.

Limitations and data gaps

  • Bolzano: trade tonnes estimated from € with Italian unit values; the use structure is borrowed from Portugal; no rail on the Brenner corridor.
  • Italy: ASIA employment does not cover agriculture, forestry and fishing (section A).
  • Coimbra: tier C (no own accounts); 15–31% of employment imputed from confidential cells; population boundary to check.
  • Sweden: DE observed only for Sweden and VGR (downscaled by jobs elsewhere, which produces small negative DE cells for the rest of Sweden); rail observed only for VGR; port transit included in NI/NO and a lower bound (Varberg SEVA1 and Trollhättan SE965 are missing from the port list); register employment jumps ~10% in 2021 (likely a definition change).
  • SE2: port 'Löten' (SELOT, 0.6 Mt domestic, 2019) could not be located and is left out.
  • Rest-of territories: gross domestic flows, production and the flush cannot be obtained by difference.
  • Model: industry inputs are estimated from imports, so inputs sourced locally can be underestimated (D1.3).
  • Years: the window differs by country (Italy 2013–2021, Portugal 2012/2015–2021, Sweden 2013–2022).
  • Bolzano 2019 is inflated by the Vaia storm windthrow (+3 Mt of roundwood). Use 2018 or 2021 when a typical year is needed.
  • Centro 2021: road freight tonnage jumps by about 60%, which affects domestic flows and DMC.
  • Rest-of territories inherit the errors of both runs. Treat small remainders (Halland, rest of VGR) with caution.
